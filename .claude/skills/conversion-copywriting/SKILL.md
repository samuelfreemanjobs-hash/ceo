# Conversion Copywriting

This skill writes copy that moves people. Not clever copy. Not award-winning copy. Copy that gets a specific reader to take a specific action at a specific stage of the funnel.

## When to invoke

- The user needs any piece of written content for the funnel
- The user has copy and wants it improved
- You're in the Build phase of the funnel workflow and need to generate stage-specific assets
- The user asks for "ideas" for ads, emails, pages, CTAs

## The prerequisite

**Do not produce copy without an audience.** If the audience-mapping skill hasn't run, the copy will be generic. Before generating, you should know:

- Who is reading this (role/identity)
- What just happened (trigger)
- What they're using now (alternative)
- Their #1 objection
- The voice/tone of the brand (extract from existing assets or ask)

If you have these, write. If you don't, stop and run audience-mapping first.

## Universal copy principles

These apply to every piece of copy this skill produces:

1. **The reader is the hero, not the product.** Use "you" 3–5× more than "we" or "our product."
2. **Specific beats clever.** "Cut your onboarding time from 2 weeks to 2 days" beats "Faster onboarding, better life."
3. **One idea per piece.** Ad, email, headline, CTA — one job each. If you're saying two things, write two pieces.
4. **Show, don't claim.** Numbers, names, screenshots, quotes — proof beats adjectives.
5. **Lead with the strongest point.** People don't read; they scan. The hook is the headline, the first line, the subject line.
6. **Pre-empt the top objection.** Whatever the audience's #1 reason for saying no is, address it where it fires.
7. **One CTA, named precisely.** "Start your free trial" beats "Get started." "Book a 15-min demo" beats "Contact us."
8. **Match channel and stage.** Cold ad copy ≠ retargeting copy ≠ trial-conversion email copy. Same audience, different state.

## The copy formulas

Pick a formula based on what you're writing. These are scaffolds — don't follow them mechanically.

### PAS (Problem → Agitate → Solve)

Best for: cold ads, top-of-funnel content, problem-aware audiences.

```
PROBLEM:   "Your team's standups have become 30-minute status meetings."
AGITATE:   "Engineers are tuning out. The questions get vague. Blockers stay hidden until Friday."
SOLVE:     "Async standups in Slack. Three questions, 60 seconds, no meeting."
CTA:       "Start your free 14-day trial."
```

When to use: when the audience already feels the pain and just needs to recognize it in your words. When to avoid: when the audience doesn't yet know they have the problem — leads with too much "you're doing it wrong" energy.

### BAB (Before → After → Bridge)

Best for: landing pages, sales pages, transformation-led offers.

```
BEFORE:    "Your standups eat 45 minutes a day, every day, and people are checked out."
AFTER:     "Your team writes 60-second updates in Slack. You read them with coffee. Mornings are yours."
BRIDGE:    "[Product] turns daily standups into 90-second async check-ins your team will actually do."
CTA:       "See how it works in 2 minutes."
```

When to use: when transformation is visible and concrete. Best with strong before/after framing. When to avoid: when "after" is abstract or hard to picture.

### AIDA (Attention → Interest → Desire → Action)

Best for: long-form sales pages, video sales letters, traditional direct response.

```
ATTENTION: Bold headline that stops the scroll. "The 9am Meeting That's Costing You $50K/Year."
INTEREST:  Establish the problem and your credibility. Why this matters now, why you to fix it.
DESIRE:    Show the outcome. Proof. Specifics. Social proof. Vision of life after.
ACTION:    Clear CTA + reduce risk (guarantee, free trial, no contract).
```

When to use: long-form sales material where you have the reader's attention. When to avoid: short-form ads or anywhere you have less than 30 seconds of attention.

### 4Ps (Promise → Picture → Proof → Push)

Best for: emails, mid-length sales copy, short landing pages.

```
PROMISE:  "Your team will write better standups in 60 seconds a day."
PICTURE:  "Engineers post from Slack on their commute. You read them over coffee. By 9:15, you know who's blocked, no meeting needed."
PROOF:    "287 engineering teams switched from live standups in Q4. Average time saved: 4.2 hours per week per manager."
PUSH:     "Try [Product] free for 14 days. No credit card. Setup takes 90 seconds."
```

When to use: anywhere you have ~150–300 words of copy real estate. When to avoid: ad copy where you have one second of attention.

### FAB (Feature → Advantage → Benefit)

Best for: feature pages, product tour copy, comparison pages.

```
FEATURE:    "Slack-native posting."
ADVANTAGE:  "No new tool to log into. Posts happen where work already happens."
BENEFIT:    "Your team actually uses it. 92% week-2 adoption vs. 34% on standalone standup tools."
```

When to use: when you have multiple features to explain and want to keep the reader focused on outcomes. When to avoid: top-of-funnel. Features at TOFU is a self-own.

## Headline archetypes

There are eight headline patterns that consistently outperform original creative ideation. Adapt these to the specific audience and offer.

1. **The specific result:** "Cut your team's daily standup time from 45 minutes to 60 seconds."
2. **The named pain:** "The 9am meeting that's quietly killing your engineering velocity."
3. **The contrarian claim:** "Stop having daily standups. (Do this instead.)"
4. **The question they're already asking:** "Why does our standup keep running 30 minutes over?"
5. **The how-to:** "How [SimilarCompany] runs standups for 80 engineers without a single meeting."
6. **The list with specificity:** "5 standup formats that actually surface blockers (and 3 that don't)."
7. **The case study hook:** "How a 22-person eng team got their mornings back."
8. **The negative reframe:** "We tried 4 standup tools. Here's what broke."

**Headline test**: a good headline can be read out loud to a stranger in the target audience, and they'd say "tell me more." If they'd say "huh?" or "so what?", rewrite.

## Email sequence architectures

Five common sequences. For full templates with worked examples, see `references/email-sequences.md`.

### Welcome sequence (5 emails, post-signup)

1. **Day 0 — Welcome + activation push.** One specific behavior that predicts retention.
2. **Day 1 — Social proof.** A customer story of someone like them succeeding.
3. **Day 3 — Common objection handled.** The thing they're probably worried about right now.
4. **Day 5 — Use case expansion.** A way to get more value than they expected.
5. **Day 7 — Soft CTA.** Trial-to-paid push, demo offer, or community invite.

### Nurture sequence (7 emails, for lead-magnet downloads)

1. Deliver the asset + set expectations.
2. Educational content #1 (relevant to lead magnet topic).
3. Educational content #2 (deepen the problem).
4. Case study or proof.
5. Soft pitch (product mentioned, not pushed).
6. Educational content #3 (introduce the solution category).
7. Direct CTA (demo, trial, consult).

### Sales sequence (4 emails, for hand-raisers)

1. **Day 0:** Trigger-relevant intro + soft offer.
2. **Day 2:** Address top objection. Add proof.
3. **Day 4:** Scarcity or specificity (limited slots, deadline, specific use case).
4. **Day 6:** Last-call email. Short, specific, easy to reply to.

### Re-engagement sequence (3 emails, for dormant)

1. "Are we still useful?" — honest check-in.
2. New value / new feature / new angle.
3. Last-chance + unsubscribe option (clean list hygiene).

### Abandoned cart / trial-abandonment (3 emails)

1. **Hour 1:** "You left something behind." Quick reminder, no pressure.
2. **Day 1:** Address the likely objection. Add social proof.
3. **Day 3:** Incentive (discount, extended trial, bonus). Final push.

## Landing page structure

For new visitor → conversion landing pages, use this skeleton:

1. **Hero** (above the fold): headline + sub-headline + primary CTA + visual.
    - Headline: specific result or named pain.
    - Sub-headline: one-line clarification of what it is and who it's for.
    - CTA: action-named, low-friction.
2. **Problem agitator** (one section): pain in the reader's words. Mirror the audience.
3. **Solution intro**: how your thing fixes it, in 3–4 sentences. Not features yet.
4. **Proof block**: 3–5 customer logos, 1–3 specific testimonials with names and outcomes, key metrics.
5. **How it works** (3 steps): simple sequence. Each step = one verb.
6. **Features → benefits** (3–5 max): each feature framed as a reader outcome.
7. **Objection handling**: FAQ or dedicated section addressing top 3 reasons not to buy.
8. **Final CTA**: same CTA as hero, reinforced. Add risk reversal (free trial, guarantee, easy cancel).

What NOT to include: every feature, every use case, multiple competing CTAs, walls of text without scannable structure.

See `references/landing-page-patterns.md` for full templates.

## CTA patterns

CTAs are where copy converts or doesn't. Rules:

- **Use a verb**: "Start", "Book", "Get", "Try", "See", "Download."
- **Name what they get**: "Start your free trial" > "Get started." "Book a 15-min walkthrough" > "Book a demo."
- **Reduce perceived risk**: "No credit card" / "Free for 14 days" / "Cancel anytime" / "5-minute setup" as supporting text under the CTA.
- **One primary CTA per page or email.** Secondary actions (learn more, see pricing) are visually subordinated.
- **Match button text to user state.** First-time visitor: "Start free trial." Returning visitor: "Resume your trial." Existing customer: "Add to your plan."

Bad CTAs that look professional but underperform: "Contact us", "Learn more", "Get in touch", "Click here", "Submit."

## Ad creative patterns

For paid social and search ads. Each has different real estate.

### Search ads (Google/Bing)

- **Headline 1**: keyword match (what they searched).
- **Headline 2**: differentiator or specific result.
- **Headline 3**: CTA or risk reversal.
- **Description**: 1–2 lines of proof + CTA.

Example for "async standup tool":

- H1: Async Standup Tool For Engineering
- H2: Cut Standups From 45 Min To 90 Sec
- H3: 14-Day Free Trial, No Card
- D: 287 eng teams ditched daily meetings. Slack-native, $29/seat. Try free for 14 days.

### Paid social ads (Meta/LinkedIn/TikTok)

- **Hook (first 3 seconds):** named pain, contrarian claim, or pattern-interrupt visual.
- **Build:** show or tell what's different. Specifics. Quick proof.
- **CTA:** clear action + risk reversal.
- **Visual + copy match.** If the visual is a product screenshot, copy mentions the feature shown. If the visual is a customer, copy uses their words.

Top performing ad angles (test each as a variant):

1. **Problem-led**: Lead with the audience pain.
2. **Result-led**: Lead with the after-state.
3. **Social-proof-led**: Lead with a customer success.
4. **Contrarian-led**: Challenge the conventional approach.
5. **Founder-led**: Personal/authentic POV from the founder.

See `references/ad-creative-patterns.md` for worked examples.

## The evaluator-optimizer loop

For high-stakes copy (hero headlines, primary CTAs, first emails, primary ad creative), run this loop:

### Step 1: Generate 3 distinct variants

Variants should be **structurally different**, not three rewordings. Try:

- One variant using the named pain (PAS opening)
- One variant using the named outcome (BAB / specific-result opening)
- One variant using social proof or contrarian framing

### Step 2: Critique each variant against these 5 questions

For each variant, answer:

1. **Hero check:** Is the reader the protagonist, or is the product the protagonist? (Should be reader.)
2. **Specificity check:** Does this contain at least one specific number, name, or detail that wouldn't apply to a competitor?
3. **Objection check:** Does this acknowledge or pre-empt the audience's top objection?
4. **Voice check:** Does this sound like the brand's voice, or like generic SaaS-speak?
5. **CTA check:** Is the next step crystal-clear, named, and low-friction?

Score each variant 1–5 on each dimension. The winning variant has the highest total AND no scores below 3.

### Step 3: Refine the winner

Take the strongest variant and improve its weakest dimensions. Don't generate a new variant — surgically improve the one that's already closest.

### Step 4: Show your work

When delivering copy to the user, show the 3 variants, the critique, and the refined winner. This makes the reasoning visible and lets the user push back on judgment calls.

## Voice calibration

Brand voice is real and matters. Before writing, calibrate by asking the user to share 2–3 examples of existing copy they like or don't like. Then identify:

- **Formality**: corporate / professional / conversational / casual / irreverent
- **Pace**: long-form / medium / punchy
- **Vocabulary**: technical / accessible / mixed
- **POV**: first-person founder / "we" company / "they" third-person
- **Humor**: none / dry / playful / absurd

When generating, match these dimensions. If the user hasn't told you, use the most likely default for the audience (B2B = professional + accessible; DTC consumer = casual + punchy; enterprise = formal + technical).

If the user hasn't shared examples, generate copy in a "neutral professional" voice and explicitly flag that voice should be tuned to brand examples before shipping.

See `references/voice-calibration.md` for the full analysis framework.

## Common copy failures (and fixes)

| Failure | Symptom | Fix |
|---------|---------|-----|
| Product as hero | "We provide cutting-edge solutions..." | Rewrite with "you" as subject. The reader is doing something, not receiving from you. |
| Adjective stacking | "Powerful, intuitive, scalable platform" | Replace each adjective with a specific. "Set up in 90 seconds." "Handles 50K events/sec." |
| Vague CTAs | "Get started" / "Learn more" | Name the action + what they get. "Start your 14-day free trial — no card needed." |
| Feature dumping | Bullet list of 12 features | Pick 3. Frame each as a reader outcome. |
| Missing objection | High traffic, low conversion | Identify top objection. Add proof or risk reversal addressing it. |
| Wrong stage | TOFU copy on a BOFU page | Match copy to stage. Pricing page ≠ blog post. |
| Generic voice | Could be any SaaS company | Add specifics. Use customer quotes. Name a competitor. |
| Buried lede | Best line is in paragraph 3 | Move it to the hook. Headline is the strongest claim. |
| Too long | 800-word ad copy | Cut by 50%. Then cut again. |

## Output checklist

When delivering copy, this skill should produce:

- [ ] The requested copy, in the right voice
- [ ] For high-stakes pieces: 3 variants + critique + refined winner
- [ ] Tags for which stage / channel this is intended for
- [ ] Flags on any assumptions (voice, audience details, etc.) that should be validated
- [ ] Next-piece-to-write if part of a sequence

## Final guidance to the agent

Bad copy is almost always a research problem in disguise. If you're struggling to generate good variants, you don't yet understand the audience well enough. Stop generating and ask one more question about the reader before continuing.

And: never deliver "draft copy" without flagging what it would take to make it shippable. The user might paste your draft straight onto a live page. Be explicit about what's verified vs. assumed, and what claims they need to fact-check before publishing.
